✓ 00 · why_this_exists · reviewed

00 · why this page exists

The rest of the series makes your machine, history, code, pipeline, and jobs trustworthy. This module is about the newest member of the team — the AI assistant — and the fact that it is a brilliant, tireless junior with no judgement, no memory of your NDA, and no name on the commit. Yours is.

This page will not make you good at using AI by itself, and it will not teach a specific tool — the tools change every month, their privacy settings change with them, and a guide pinned to one product's menus would be wrong by the next release. What it is: how these tools actually work (the model, the harness, the loop), how to get real leverage from them, and the durable questions to ask of any AI tool so a productivity boost doesn't turn into a confidentiality incident.

The frame for everything below: you are accountable for what you ship, and for what you put in the box. The AI is a tool, like a compiler with opinions. A compiler that occasionally invents a function that doesn't exist, and would happily paste your client's production password into a public model's training set if you let it.

✓ 01 · the_daily_loop · reviewed

01 · day zero: the loop

Every productive AI interaction is the same loop, and skipping a step is where the trouble starts. The two steps people drop under deadline are exactly the two that matter: check what you're about to send, and understand before you accept.

IF YOU ONLY REMEMBER THREE THINGS

① Nothing you can't show the client goes in the box — assume anything you send may be stored, logged, or trained on unless you've verified otherwise. ② You review the AI's code the way you'd review a stranger's PR — if you can't explain it, you can't merge it. ③ The agent acts; you stay the approver — keep a human between the suggestion and anything irreversible.

✓ 02 · what_it_actually_is · reviewed

02 · what these tools actually are

Modern coding assistants are large language models: systems trained on enormous amounts of text and code to predict the next token. That's the whole trick, and every strength and failure mode falls out of it. They are astonishing pattern-completers and they do not know anything — they produce the most plausible continuation, which is usually right and occasionally a confident fabrication wearing the costume of a right answer.

They arrive in roughly three shapes, in increasing order of power and risk:

  • Autocomplete (inline "ghost text") — completes the line or block as you type. Lowest friction, easiest to accept on autopilot.
  • Chat — you paste context and ask; it answers. You control exactly what goes in, which is the point and the trap.
  • Agents — given a goal, they read files, run commands, edit code, and loop until done. Highest leverage, and the only category that can take destructive action on your behalf.

The honest history: this went from "neat autocomplete" to "writes the whole function" to "runs your terminal" in about three years, which is why the tooling is powerful, the defaults are immature, and the security guidance — this page included — is still being written in real time. Treat confident claims about AI tools (again, this page included) as provisional, and verify the ones that touch confidential data against the tool's current documentation.

the model is not the tool: model vs harness

Two things get conflated, and keeping them apart explains most of how these tools behave. The model is the token-predictor — the engine. The harness is everything wrapped around it: the loop that lets it call tools, run commands, read files, fetch URLs, and feed the results back so it can take another step. Claude Code, Codex, and IDE agents are harnesses; the model is the part inside that does the thinking.

This matters because most of what makes an agent useful — or dangerous — lives in the harness, not the model: which tools it's allowed to call, whether it asks before acting, what it pulls into context, how it handles a filling context window. When you "choose an AI tool" or "lock down its settings," you are mostly choosing and configuring a harness. The same model is a polite autocomplete in one harness and an agent with your shell in another.

THE THREE SHAPES, ONE RULE

Autocomplete → chat → agent is really "more harness": more tools, more autonomy, more context gathered for you. You gain leverage and lose oversight by default. The discipline is identical at every level, just more important the further right you go: know what went in, and review what comes out.

✓ 03 · mental_model · the eager junior

03 · the mental model: a brilliant junior with no accountability

Naive model: "It's a search engine that writes code — if it said it, it's probably right." Real model: it's an extremely fast, widely-read, supremely confident junior engineer who only knows what's in front of them right now, will never be blamed, doesn't know your NDA exists, and cannot tell you when they're guessing. You are the senior who reviews, signs off, and owns the outcome.

Hold all four of the junior's traits at once and most decisions make themselves. Fast and well-read: lean on it for boilerplate, unfamiliar syntax, first drafts, and rubber-ducking. Only knows what's in its context: the model has no inherent, persistent knowledge of your codebase. A plain chat has seen nothing of it; an agent or IDE tool (Claude Code, Codex, an indexed assistant) can read it — but only the parts it retrieved or that fit the window, and it forgets at the session's end. So it still guesses at internal APIs whenever the relevant code isn't actually in front of it — give it the real code, and verify against yours. Won't be blamed: the accountability is entirely yours, so the review is not optional. Can't tell when it's guessing: a fabricated library, function, or fact reads exactly as confidently as a correct one — this is called hallucination, and it is the failure mode that ships bugs.

THE ACCOUNTABILITY RULE

"The AI wrote it" is not a defence — not in code review, not in a security post-mortem, not to a client. The moment you accept a suggestion it is your code, with your name in git blame. Use the AI to write faster; never to understand less.

✓ 04 · core_reference · prompt + review

04 · core reference: prompting and reviewing

get more out of it without a pro subscription

The biggest efficiency lever is not the tier — it's the prompt and the context. A free model with a good prompt beats a frontier model with a vague one. What actually moves the needle, in order:

  • State the goal, the constraints, and the shape of the answer. "Rewrite this function to stream instead of buffering; keep the signature; Python 3.12; no new dependencies" gets a usable answer the first time. "make this better" burns a turn.
  • Give it the relevant code, not the repo. The right two functions and a type beat ten files of noise — better answers and less to leak (see §05).
  • Show one example of the pattern you want. One sample input/output or one "do it like this" snippet outperforms paragraphs of description.
  • Iterate in the same thread. "Now handle the empty case" is cheaper and better than re-describing from scratch.
  • Ask it to explain, not just produce. "Explain the trade-offs first, then write it" turns the review step into part of the answer — and catches the confident-wrong answer early.
THE FREE-TIER PLAYBOOK

Most of the gain is available free: use generous free tiers for boilerplate, explanation, and unfamiliar syntax; reserve any paid seat (if you have one) for the long-context, whole-codebase work that free tiers throttle. The constraint that actually decides your tool is what you're allowed to put in it — not the price.

the review discipline — treat it like a stranger's PR

You already have the checklist: it's the git guide's self-review, pointed at code you didn't write. Specifically, before you accept:

checkthe AI-specific failure it catches
Does it run, and do the tests pass?plausible code that doesn't actually execute
Do these functions/libraries exist?hallucinated APIs and packages (also a supply-chain risk — see §07)
Can I explain every line?subtly wrong logic hiding in confident, clean-looking code
Does it match our conventions and real APIs?the model guessed at internal code it didn't actually have in context
Any secret, path, or client detail baked in?credentials and PII the model "helpfully" hardcoded

the many faces of "context"

"Context" is whatever the tool sends to the model — and it's often more than you typed. Knowing what each mode sends is the whole game for §05.

modewhat it sends to the modelhow much you control

the agent loop, and where you stand in it

An agent doesn't answer once and stop — it loops. Given a goal, it plans a step, acts (calls a tool: edits a file, runs a command, fetches a page), observes the result, and decides the next step, repeating until it judges the goal met or gets stuck. That loop is the whole reason agents can fix their own mistakes — they see the error and try again — and the whole reason they can run off a cliff at speed.

Two consequences shape how you work with the loop. First, the context window is the agent's working memory and it's finite — every step (files read, commands run, output) fills it. Long, sprawling sessions fill it up; the harness then compacts or summarizes, and detail quietly rots, which is why a marathon agent run drifts from what you asked. The fix is to scope tasks small enough that one loop finishes before it forgets, and start a fresh session for the next thing rather than letting one run for an hour. Second, a loop needs an exit it can check: "make this test pass" terminates and is verifiable; "improve the codebase" never terminates and can't be reviewed. Give it a goal with a green light at the end.

prompt vs skill vs harness — what to put where

When the AI keeps doing a thing slightly wrong, the fix lives at one of three layers, and naming the layer saves you from solving it in the wrong place:

layerwhat it isreach for it when…
prompt / contextthe one-off ask for this task — the instructions and code you hand it nowit's a single task; you just need to be clearer or give the real code
skilla named, reusable bundle of instructions (sometimes with scripts) the agent loads when a recurring task appears — "how we write migrations," "our PR checklist"you've pasted the same procedure three times; encode it once so the agent applies it consistently without you re-explaining
harnessthe loop, the tools it can call, and the permissions it runs under — the runtime you configure, not write per taskyou're fighting what it's allowed to do (too much access, no approval step, can't see the right files) — that's config, not prompting
THE TELL

If you're re-explaining the same workflow to the AI every week, that's a skill, not a prompt — promote it. If you're repeatedly talking it out of doing something dangerous, that's the harness — fix the permissions, don't rely on asking nicely. Prompting harder is the right fix only for genuinely one-off work.

⚠ 05 · what_goes_in_the_box · read this twice

05 · what goes in the box: not leaking the client

This is the section the whole module exists for. As a consultant you are handling someone else's confidential code and data under an NDA, and an AI tool is a pipe to a third party. The cardinal sin is putting client-confidential material into a tool that retains, logs, or trains on it — because once it's in someone else's model or training set, you cannot get it back, and you have breached the NDA whether or not anything visibly "happens."

🚫 THE BRIGHT LINE

If you couldn't paste it into a stranger's public website, don't paste it into an AI tool you haven't verified. Client source code, real data, customer PII, credentials, internal URLs, architecture details, unreleased plans — all of it is "client confidential" by default. When unsure, treat it as confidential.

the three questions to ask of any tool, before any client data touches it

  1. Retention: does it store my inputs, and for how long? "Zero data retention" is the phrase to look for; "30-day abuse-monitoring logs" is not zero.
  2. Training: does it train on my inputs? Consumer/free tiers often do by default; business, enterprise, and API tiers usually don't — but this is a setting and a contract term, not a vibe. Verify both.
  3. Tenancy & contract: is there an agreement that covers confidential data (a DPA, a zero-retention API arrangement, an enterprise term)? For a regulated client, "I toggled a setting" is weaker than "there's a signed term."

Crucially, the client decides, not you. Many engagements have an explicit AI policy — sanctioned tools, approved data classes, an outright ban on certain repos. Find it before you start. Where there's no policy, the safe default is: assume client material may not go into external AI tools until someone with authority says which tool and which data are approved, in writing.

how to get the help without sending the secret

▶ leaks: real context pasted whole 
# pasted into a public chat tool:
"why does this fail?
conn = connect(
  'postgresql://svc_nimbus:Pr0d!9x@'
  'nimbus-prod.internal:5432/billing')
# ...400 lines of client code..."
✓ same help, nothing confidential 
# reduced to the shape of the problem:
"why might a psycopg2 connection
 time out only under load?
 conn string + creds via env var,
 pooled, ~200 concurrent."
# no creds, no host, no client code

The techniques, cheapest first: reproduce the problem in a tiny neutral snippet (usually the fastest path anyway); redact names, hosts, keys, and data to placeholders; describe the shape instead of pasting the thing; and for the cases where you genuinely need real code on real data, use a tool the client has approved for it.

the escape hatch: keep it on your machine

When the work genuinely needs confidential code and you can't send it anywhere, run a local or self-hosted model — the weights run on your hardware (or the client's), and nothing leaves the box. Capabilities trail the frontier hosted models and it costs you RAM and setup, but for "I cannot legally transmit this," local inference is the answer that makes the question disappear. It's also the one option whose privacy you can fully verify: no network, no retention, no contract to read.

THE BOUNDARY RULE (again — it's the whole series)

Validate the outside world at the door; here, decide at the door what may leave your machine. The setup guide says secrets live in .env and never in git; this guide adds: and never in a prompt. Same instinct, new pipe.

06 · the settings that keep you safe

Discipline you have to remember fails under deadline; settings and gates don't. The tool-agnostic checklist — find the equivalent in whatever you use, and verify, don't assume:

setting / gatewhat it preventswhere
turn OFF "train on my data" / improve-the-modelyour input entering a training setaccount & privacy settings; often on by default on free tiers
prefer a zero-retention / business / API tier for client workinputs stored in logs you don't controlplan choice + the provider's data terms
scope what autocomplete & indexing can readsilent upload of files you didn't mean to sharean ignore file (e.g. .aiignore/.cursorignore); disable indexing on sensitive repos
secret-scanning pre-commit hookan AI-suggested or pasted key reaching the repogitleaks in pre-commit — see the git guide
run agents sandboxed / least-privilegea destructive or injected command hitting real systemscontainers, scoped tokens, no prod creds in the agent's env
require approval for agent actionsirreversible steps taken without youthe tool's "ask before running / applying" mode — keep it on

updates: control them, don't fear them and don't auto-trust them

The instinct to "turn off auto-updates for security" is half-right and, taken literally, dangerous — blocking updates is how you end up running the version with the public CVE. The actual goal is control, not abstinence:

  • Don't auto-update blindly — a silent update can change what data the tool sends, add a feature that indexes your repo, or pull in a compromised dependency. Updates that change behaviour deserve a glance at the changelog.
  • Don't block security patches — pin and review, then apply. "Never update" trades a hypothetical bad update for a certain known vulnerability.
  • Vet extensions and MCP servers like dependencies — every editor extension and every agent connector runs with your access. Install from sources you'd trust with your credentials, check what permissions they ask for, and prefer few well-known ones over many convenient unknowns. This is the setup guide's "pin everything, vet your tools" applied to a surface that can read your whole project and call out to the network.
DATABRICKS / ENTERPRISE REALITY

On a managed platform or under a corporate AI policy, most of these are decided for you — sanctioned models, retention terms, and network egress are set at the org level. Your job there is to stay inside the sanctioned path and not route around it with a personal account because it's faster. The shadow-AI workaround is the incident.

✗ 07 · the_most_wanted · 8 rejected

07 · the most wanted

Eight habits that turn an AI productivity boost into an incident report. All have a commit with your name on it.

★ WANTED ★🙈

The Blind Accepter

Crime: Tab-accepts every suggestion without reading it. Ships code they can't explain; finds out what it does when it pages them.

THE FIXRead before you accept. If you can't explain a line in review, you can't merge it — AI or not.
★ WANTED ★🔑

The Secret Paster

Crime: pastes a stack trace — with the live connection string in it — into a public chat tool "just to debug quickly".

THE FIXRedact before you paste. The key is now compromised: rotate it (see SOS). Better: env vars, never in the text you send.
★ WANTED ★📂

The Context Dumper

Crime: pastes the entire client repo for a one-function question. Worse answers, and a wholesale NDA breach in one Ctrl-V.

THE FIXSend the minimal relevant snippet. Less to leak, and the model answers better with less noise.
★ WANTED ★👻

The Hallucination Shipper

Crime: merged a call to pandas.read_parquet_fast(). It doesn't exist. Neither did the package the AI told them to pip install.

THE FIXVerify functions and packages exist before using them — hallucinated package names are also a supply-chain attack vector ("slopsquatting").
★ WANTED ★🤖

The Unleashed Agent

Crime: gave an agent prod credentials and "auto-approve all," went to lunch. Came back to a migration it ran on the wrong database.

THE FIXSandbox agents, scoped tokens, no prod creds in reach, approval required for irreversible actions.
★ WANTED ★🎣

The Injection Victim

Crime: pointed an agent at a web page / issue / file that contained hidden instructions. The agent followed them, not the user.

THE FIXTreat all fetched content as untrusted data, not commands. Don't give agents both sensitive access and exposure to arbitrary content.
★ WANTED ★🚪

The Shadow-AI Smuggler

Crime: the client's tool was slow, so they used a personal account on the real code "just this once." The policy existed for a reason.

THE FIXStay inside the sanctioned path. If it's too slow, raise it — routing around the policy IS the incident.
★ WANTED ★🔄

The Blind Auto-Updater

Crime: auto-updates everything instantly OR refuses every update on principle. One ships a compromised extension; the other runs the known CVE.

THE FIXControl updates: review changelogs, apply security patches, vet extensions and MCP servers like the dependencies they are.

⧖ 08 · the_eternal_debate · your turn

08 · the eternal debate: let it drive, or keep your hands on the wheel

🏎️ full autonomy

"Give the agent the goal and let it run — read, edit, test, loop. Reviewing every step throws away the whole speed advantage."

Fails when: it confidently does the wrong thing fast, on real systems, and you're accountable for a change you never saw.

✋ human-in-the-loop

"Approve each meaningful step. The AI drafts; I decide. Slower per action, but nothing irreversible happens without me."

Fails when: applied so rigidly to trivial, reversible work that you're rubber-stamping and lose the productivity entirely.

THE RULE — calibrate autonomy to blast radius

Let it run freely where mistakes are cheap and reversible (a scratch branch, a sandbox, generated tests you'll read) — and keep a hand on the wheel where they aren't (anything touching prod, real data, credentials, money, or a destructive command). The dial isn't "trust the AI" vs "don't"; it's "how much does it cost if this step is wrong?" Same logic as the git guide's force-push rule: freedom on your own branch, ceremony on shared/irreversible ground.

✗ 09 · incident_recovery · SOS

09 · SOS — it already happened

Breathe. Most of these are recoverable if you act in the right order — and the right order almost always puts containment before cleanup. Find your scenario.

"I pasted a client secret / credential into an AI tool"

  1. Rotate the credential now — before anything else. The moment it left your machine, assume it's compromised; revoke and reissue it. This is the same rule as the git guide's leaked-secret recipe: rotation is the fix, everything else is hygiene.
  2. Delete the conversation / data if the tool allows, and check whether your tier retained or trained on it.
  3. Tell someone. Under an NDA, a pasted secret may be a reportable event — your lead and the client's policy decide, not you. Quiet hoping is the worst option; disclosure is recoverable, cover-up isn't.

"I pasted client code/data into a public tool"

You can't un-send it. Don't compound it by deleting evidence or staying silent. Capture what was sent, when, and to which tool; check the tool's retention/training terms; and escalate per the engagement's policy. The honest, early report is the professional move and usually the recoverable one.

"An agent ran a destructive command"

  • Stop the agent and cut its access (revoke the token it's using) before assessing.
  • Assess blast radius from the command log — agents log what they ran; read it bottom-up like a traceback.
  • Recover from backups / git — and note this is precisely why agents get scoped tokens, sandboxes, and no prod creds. The fix is mostly preventive, in §06.

"I shipped AI code I didn't understand and it broke"

git revert the change (the git guide's polite undo), then read what it actually did — slowly, this time. The lesson isn't "AI bad"; it's that the review step is load-bearing. Add the test that would have caught it before you re-ship.

"The agent did something I never asked — prompt injection"

It read instructions hidden in content it fetched (a web page, an issue, a file, a dependency's README) and treated them as commands. Contain as with any rogue agent: stop it, revoke access, assess from the log. Then fix the exposure — an agent should never have both sensitive access and an open pipe to arbitrary untrusted content. Treat everything the agent reads as data, never as instructions.

what happenedrecoverable?first move

🚨 THE GOLDEN RULE

Containment before cleanup, disclosure before cover-up. For a leak, rotate/revoke first and tell someone early — an NDA incident reported in the first hour is a manageable problem; the same incident discovered later, unreported, is a trust failure. You cannot un-send data, but you can always control what you do in the next five minutes.

bonus weapon: the dry run 🔬

# before letting an agent loose on anything that matters, make the cost of a mistake ~zero:
git switch -c ai/experiment   # throwaway branch — blast radius is one `git branch -D`
# point the agent at a sandbox / local DB, with a read-only or sandbox-scoped token.
# let it run, then review the diff + command log as one PR — accept on your terms.
git diff main...HEAD          # the whole of what it did, in one reviewable view
⧖ 10 · boss_fight · 0/5

10 · boss fight 🐉

Five questions between you and the rank of Engineer of Record. The commit will have your name on it either way.

1/5 · You're debugging a failure in client code. The fastest safe way to ask an AI?

Minimal, redacted context leaks nothing and usually gets a better answer. "Please don't store this" is not a control — the tool's tier and terms are. b/d still send the confidential data.

2/5 · The AI suggests import quickparse and tells you to pip install quickparse. You…

Hallucinated package names are a real supply-chain attack ("slopsquatting") — attackers register the names AIs invent. Verify before you install anything an AI named.

3/5 · "Should I turn off auto-updates on my AI tools for security?"

Blocking updates leaves you on the known-CVE version; blind auto-update can pull a compromised extension. The goal is controlled, reviewed updates — not abstinence.

4/5 · An agent fetched a web page that contained hidden text telling it to email your repo to an address. It tried to. This is…

Classic prompt injection. Everything an agent reads is untrusted data, not commands. Don't pair sensitive access with exposure to arbitrary content; require approval for outbound actions.

5/5 · You accepted AI code, shipped it, and it broke prod. In the post-mortem, "the AI wrote it" is…

Accepting a suggestion makes it yours, with your name in git blame. The AI writes faster; it never makes you understand less. The review is the job.
